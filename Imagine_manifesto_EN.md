# The September 15 Manifesto

## An open letter to the people of Honduras

---

### I. The call

Hondurans:

Honduras is rich in honest, capable, inspiring people. They are in the clinics, in the classrooms, in the workshops, in the fields, and scattered across the entire world. To the finest of this country, inside it and beyond it, we offer these ideas.

And we ask one thing before anything else: imagine. Everything that exists was imagined first. No school, no bridge, no law ever began as anything but an image in the head of someone who dared to describe it out loud.

So let us imagine. Children who do not sleep in the street, armies of peace, clean rivers and abundant water for all. None of that requires a miracle. All of it is a decision we have not yet made.

For those who can imagine it, these ideas will be like a seed. The decision to make them real will need successive governments to water them and citizens who tend their growth.

You will notice this letter carries no name. It is on purpose. A text with no owner deserved authors with no name. It does not matter who wrote this: what matters is who has imagination enough, and courage enough, to live it.

### II. What we see

Honduras spends **L 15,300 million a year** on its armed forces. It is the largest military budget in Central America, in one of the poorest countries, and it has grown **140% since 2014**, while poverty barely moved. At the same time, **between 18,000 and 20,000 children** barely survive on our streets, and the state institution responsible for protecting them operates on some **L 366 million a year**. Forty times more for weapons than for childhood. That is to say: for every child living on the street, the military budget amounts to **L 765,000 a year**, several times what it would cost to give each one social support, health care, a home, a school.

The Presidency also administers an annual confidential account. In the national budget for 2025 this was Object of Expenditure #449, at **L 12,171 million**, a 35% increase over the previous year. It is almost as large as the entire military budget and thirty-three times the childhood budget. It was spent in full. Nobody was told what for.

And every year the state forgives **nearly L 77,000 million in tax exemptions**, 8.4% of everything the country produces, among the highest rates in Latin America. Meanwhile, 66% of what is actually collected comes from indirect taxes, above all the sales tax we all pay in corner shops and supermarkets.

None of this is by accident. It is the product of decisions made by those who have had the chance to govern. But it is reversible. Better decisions follow when priorities are genuinely guided by courage, creativity, and love of one's neighbor.

No one has to take away a soldier's wage to pay for what follows. The money already exists: it is in that account nobody explains and in those privileges nobody justifies.

### III. Childhood: the unconditional commitment

We ask the country to make this its priority, a commitment without conditions, without negotiable deadlines, and without fine print: **no child will sleep or work begging in the street. Orphans abandoned at a traffic light is unacceptable.**

First in every national budget: homes, caregivers, schools, and health care for the 20,000 children of our streets, and effective help for every person at social risk.

There is another childhood almost no one names. In the municipal dumps whole families live on top of the garbage, and their children do not go to school: they sort waste in order to eat. We will close those dumps, as explained further on, and those families will be the first trained and hired, with wages and with dignity, into the system that replaces them.

### IV. Peace: the transformation of the army

We propose that Honduras declare itself a pacific nation, and prove it with the boldest act in its history: the conversion of its army from an institution of arms into an institution of service to nature and to life.

We do not propose abolition. We propose transformation. Ask anyone what they have ever thanked a soldier for. It was not their weapons. It was the boats in the floods, the rescues, the shelters. Mitch, Eta, and Iota made that plain. Let that stop being the exception and become the mission: disaster-response brigades, engineering corps for reconstruction, guardians of forests and rivers. For this we do not need weapons.

To the soldiers of Honduras we say: no one will lose their livelihood. They will keep their salary or a better one, their ranks and their honors. The only thing that changes is the mission, and it changes to one they can tell their children about with pride: no longer guarding weapons, but guarding life.

Military service will become **civic service**, open to every young person in the country: in the rivers, in the forests, in the schools, in the rescue teams. Honduras abolished forced conscription in 1994 and does well not to go back. This service does not draft: it invites. It will be voluntary and paid, with a scholarship, and it will carry preference afterward in public employment. We will compel no one. We will make it so worthwhile that few will want to be left out.

And who defends us then? The same thing that defends our neighbors today: treaties, international law, and regional cooperation. Costa Rica has had no army since 1948 and Panama since 1990, and neither has fared worse for it.

Let no one call this a luxury. Our forests, our rivers, our reefs, and our biodiversity are the most valuable capital Honduras owns, and the only kind that produces wealth forever if protected: water, fertile soil, fisheries, tourism, air. A country that spends millions guarding weapons while leaving its forests unguarded has its inventory backwards. Protecting those assets is the foundation of a sustainable economy, with jobs that do not run out.

There is also an urgent reason. The climate has already changed, and Honduras is, year after year, among the countries hardest hit in the world by extreme weather. The science is clear: stronger hurricanes are coming. Adapting is not optional. That is why the rescue and prevention brigades will not be an ornament of this transformation: they will be its backbone. Early warning in every valley, shelters ready, teams trained before the storm and not after. And by mitigating we can also lead: a country that pollutes almost nothing can be among the first to reach net-zero emissions, strengthening its economy along the way.

### V. Disarmament

The army of Honduras holds an arsenal that, with this transformation, it will no longer need. Those weapons will be dismantled in stages, without a haste that puts anyone at risk and without pauses that turn the promise into one more announcement.

None will be resold. None will pass into other hands, inside or outside the country: the weapon Honduras stops using cannot end up killing somewhere else. They will be melted down, and with that metal we will build bridges.

The bridges will be made of that metal, raised partly by the hands of those who surrendered the weapons, and the rivers will run beneath them, clean.

### VI. The new police

Honduras needs a police force it can trust. That requires paying what trust costs.

Every current officer will pass through vetting and recertification: asset verification, evaluation, intensive retraining. Those who pass will see their salary at least tripled, from today's 15,000 lempiras a month to a wage that transforms their family's life. New entrants will face stricter requirements, beginning with education level. Being police will be a prestigious profession.

This is not a gift: it is what is fair. We ask an officer to stand between us and danger, to work through the night, to go in first where no one wants to go, and on top of that to refuse every lempira anyone offers them. That kind of integrity cannot be demanded on a wage that does not last the fortnight. To those we place on the front line we owe a decent salary, health coverage, and a pension for their family if they do not come home. A country that pays its police well buys two things at once: courage and honesty.

That police force, equipped with training and technology, will also advance toward patrolling without lethal weapons. This is not an untested idea: in the United Kingdom, Norway, Ireland and New Zealand ordinary police patrol unarmed, and for extreme cases there are small, strictly controlled specialized units. That is the model: the officer on the corner without a pistol, and behind them a unit trained for the exceptional, answerable to a judge for every shot fired. Disarming the officer on the corner does not leave them defenseless. Non-lethal technology already stops, knocks down and immobilizes an aggressor without taking a life; whoever attacks will end up on the ground, but breathing, and then before a judge. It will be done in stages, with measurable violence-reduction milestones. Tripling police salaries costs some L 7,100 million a year: little more than half of what goes today, unexplained, through the secret account. We can afford it.

### VII. Territory: forests, rivers, waste, energy

**The forests.** Every protected area will have permanent guardians, not just paperwork. The civic service brigades will reforest the watersheds that give us water, and illegal logging will stop being a low-risk business. A standing forest gives water, rain, legal timber and work every year; a felled forest gives money once.

**The rivers.** We will recover clean rivers running through our urban centers, with those same brigades. The flagship river will be the Choluteca: running clean through Tegucigalpa and Comayagüela, it will be a source of pride again. Cities immensely larger than Tegucigalpa keep their rivers this way. We have no valid excuses.

**The waste.** Universal collection, separation at the source, and the closure of open-air dumps, with formal employment for the families who survive in them today. The law ordering their closure has existed since 2020: what never arrived was the budget or the will. And a national campaign against litter, because the bag that clogs the drain is next winter's flood, and that chain will be taught in every school. On the horizon, a target that seems impossible: a plant that turns waste into energy, like CopenHill in Copenhagen. Read about it. It cost some 670 million dollars, what was spent here, unexplained, in a year and a half of the secret account. The impossible goal costs what we were already spending without knowing on what.

**The energy.** Honduras already generates most of its electricity from renewable sources. We propose extending that strength to every family's rooftop: a national industry of affordable roofs combining solar panels with rainwater harvesting. One object against four poverties — of energy, of water, of housing, and of employment — because the design, assembly, installation, and maintenance will be Honduran trades.

### VIII. The open school

We want the school to replace the street. Let the building be open twelve hours a day and be the heart of every community: classroom, cafeteria, library, place to play, safe place. Eight of those hours will be class; the rest, sport, homework, music and community, with the doors open to the neighbors as well.

The school snack already exists and reaches more than a million children, but every year it depends on agreements and donors. We will make it a permanent obligation of the state and turn it into a hot lunch, every day.

On full-day schooling, the current government has announced that from 2027 students will be in class from seven in the morning to three in the afternoon. We applaud it. That is three more hours of learning a day, three fewer hours on the street at precisely the hours when the maras recruit, and the door through which lunch enters the school. It is also the kind of opportunity this country has let die many times: in paperwork, in a change of government, in a budget that never arrived. Not this time. We commit to seeing it carried out, funded and sustained, whoever governs.

### IX. Health: unrestricted access

Health will be a right of every Honduran, not a privilege of those who can pay for it. Today, nearly nine in ten people in Honduras lack coverage. We propose universal, unrestricted access to the health system.

And we know a card cures no one. That is why the first level comes first: health centers open and stocked, with a doctor, a nurse and medicines, near where people live, so the hospital stops being the only door.

For children, access will be unconditional, always. A child's doctor will never depend on an adult's paperwork. For adults, enrollment will run through the tax registry: the tax number will be the door through which one enters the health system, not a wall that keeps one out. Whoever lacks documents will be helped to obtain them, not turned away. A single procedure will make each person now in the informal economy visible to the health system and to the tax system. Access will draw people toward order and formality.

### X. Taxes

This letter calls everyone aboard, and coming aboard means contributing.

But the state must first earn its taxes before raising them. So we propose a sequence: for the first two years, no major tax reform. Two years for the country to see results: the children off the streets, the police transformed, violence in decline. The only thing falling from day one will be the privileges: the dismantling of the exemption regime that gives away L 77,000 million a year. Before asking anyone to pay more, we will stop allowing a few to pay nothing.

The reform will be drafted in public during those two years, debated in full view, and take effect in year three: more on high income and idle wealth, less on the basic basket. And since a single term is not enough to see it mature, it will go into law, with written targets and deadlines, so that the next government has to sustain it or repeal it in the open.

### XI. The open door

On violence, this letter distinguishes between the maras and the narco.

To the thousands of young people trapped in the maras we offer **the Open Door**. Whoever crosses it will leave the mara behind, confess before their victims in a restorative justice process, and enter the civic service. The one who terrorized a neighborhood will spend their days raising bridges, schools and forests. Serious crimes are not amnestied. They are reduced through confession and reparation, not erased, and it is always a judge who decides, with the victim present and heard. The commitment to childhood in chapter III is this letter's deepest anti-mara policy, because the maras recruit precisely among those 20,000 children we must rescue.

We know that many look to El Salvador today and want the same here. It is understandable: people want to walk in peace. But full prisons do not touch the port where the drugs come in, nor the file that goes missing, nor the fortune nobody explains. Honduras is not sick only with street crime and extortion: it is sick with corruption, and against that no prison is enough. There is something else that does not appear in the statistics: a state that can lock up a guilty person without evidence can also lock up an innocent one, and one day whoever thinks differently.

On the drug trade, we declare no new war. As long as billions of dollars are waiting on the other side, someone will carry them, and that demand is not in our hands. What is in our hands is enough: to make the business slower, costlier and riskier, until operating from Honduras stops being worth it.

So we will pursue the illicit money invested in land, in cattle, in hotels, in construction firms. That is where the breath is cut off. And there is no need to invent the tool: Honduras already has a law for the forfeiture of assets of illicit origin and a financial intelligence unit. What is missing is using them, with prosecutors, with resources, and without phone calls that stop the files. We will modernize customs and ports. We will ask our international partners to share financial intelligence before military intelligence: drugs can be hidden, money leaves a trail.

We will also change how success is measured. Instead of announcing kilos seized and kingpins fallen, we will tell the country how much illicit wealth was recovered, how much extortion fell, and how many people trust their police again.

### XII. The covenant of trust

And who will do all this? Those who step forward. But of whoever does, the country must demand guarantees, because in a land intoxicated with corruption, trust is not requested. It is earned.

**Whoever leads will be independent of every party.** And will present to the country, **before the election, the complete cabinet**. A team will be elected, not a person. Each prospective minister will publicly sign the chapter of this letter they are to execute.

**The leader will voluntarily cap their wealth**: full disclosure, blind trust, limited salary. And will serve **a single term. They will leave on time.** No maneuvers, no friendly judges, no exceptions. A leader capped in wealth and capped in time. The continuity will not be a man or a woman: it will be a program like this one, the team that signs it, and the governments with the decency to continue it.

**The ministers, by contrast, will earn openly competitive salaries**, because the country deserves to hire its best, and underpaid power seeks payment by other means. Cheap government is the most expensive thing Honduras has ever bought.

**The judges will not be negotiated.** The Supreme Court and the Public Prosecutor will be chosen by open competition, with hearings broadcast live on national television and the public watching, not in late-night meetings between parties.

**The books will be open.** First decree: the elimination of the Presidency's confidential account. The Executive will hold not one lempira the people cannot see. Should any security operation require temporary reserve, it will be minimal and audited by an independent body: reserve from the public, never from oversight.

**The campaign will be clean and without a price.** Whoever embraces this letter will not buy a vote, a shirt, or a minute of airtime. Their only currency will be the volunteered time of free citizens: word of mouth, whatever media choose to cover it, the social networks. And on winning, they will restructure party financing — transparent, capped, criminally enforced — and renounce any public financing that might correspond to them: every lempira that reaches their hands will go, in full, to the program for removing the children from the streets.

### XIII. The measure of success

And how will we know if any of this works? We will not measure it in what we produce. We will measure it in how we live.

There is a global index that asks people, country by country, how good they consider their own lives: the World Happiness Report. Honduras today stands 63rd among 147 nations. Hidden in that number is a miracle: we score *above the world average*. After everything we have done to this country, Honduran joy still survives. Imagine what it would do if we governed in its favor.

There is one more measure no index records and everyone understands: how many people stop leaving. Every chapter of this letter is, at bottom, an argument for staying. No one should cross three borders to feed their children. Those who have already gone this letter counts as Hondurans in full: today they may vote only for president, and we will ask that they also vote for their Congress and their mayor, because those who sustain this country with what they send every month deserve a say in it. And those who return deported, often with nothing but what they are wearing, are owed something better than a photograph at the airport: a place in the civic service, a trade, an open door.

So we propose the final challenge: that Honduras become the first country to declare the happiness of its people its official measure of progress. That every year, on September 15, the government account not for gross domestic product, but for this: how many children remain on the streets, how clean the rivers run, how much the people trust their police and their judges, how many stayed, and how happy the Honduran people are, in data and not in speeches. Money is a means. It always was. The good life is the end.

### XIV. The renunciation

This letter demands much of those who embrace it: capped wealth, a single term, campaigns without money, open books. It cannot demand anything it does not practice first. And so it strips itself of everything.

It has no authors. It has no owner. It has no party, no headquarters, no board of directors, no bank account. It accepts no donations. It fields no candidates. It charges no membership. It does not even register the signatures: there is no office to deliver them to, because there is no office.

Who are we, then? We will not tell you our names, but we owe you this much: we are a husband and wife, with two small children, and we both work for a salary. We own no house; we rent our roof. The printed newspaper page cost us more than L 23,000 and the web page another L 8,500. We paid for all of it ourselves, out of savings our family cannot easily spare. If we did not believe all of this is possible, we would not have spent a single lempira. We seek nothing in return. Whatever this idea does from here on, it will do in your hands or not at all. We wrote it out of love: for our family, for this planet, and for the possibility that a small, battered country might teach the world what peace looks like when it is taken seriously. Because this idea is not only for Honduras. If Honduras achieves it, it will be a model for the world.

### XV. The signature

To those who govern today, and those who aspire to govern tomorrow: **sign this, or explain to the country, point by point and face to face, why not**.

To those who step forward we say: wait for no party's permission. Honduran law allows independent candidacies for the presidency, for Congress, and for the mayorships, with enough signatures from your department. It will not be easy: the law demands tens of thousands of signatures and no one has managed it yet. That is why it must begin early and everywhere. Let independent candidates rise in all eighteen departments with this letter in hand, to win a renewed Congress.

Of every Honduran, here or in the diaspora, we ask something simple: when someone takes that step, back them. Sign for them when they ask for the signatures the law demands. Pass this letter to whoever you can. And when the day comes, vote. That is all. No more than that is needed from each of us to change everything.

Independence was proclaimed on a September 15th. The republic, the real one, the one that seeks equal opportunity for each and every one before a single law, still has a pending date.

Some may say we are dreamers. But we are not the only ones.

We propose to begin founding it today.

Whoever wishes to sign, sign in your mind, from the heart. That is enough. The paper is worth nothing, what we do about it is worth everything.
